# Before you download a dataset, write down the question

Research by Aaron Michael Weis · [ORCID](https://orcid.org/0009-0002-1906-148X)

Imagine a neighborhood group trying to decide which streets need a tree count this summer. Someone finds a city inventory. It has coordinates, species names, and more rows than anyone expected. Two questions could change whether the group uses it at all. When did the surveyors last walk those streets? Did they count trees in front yards as well as trees along the curb?

*Choosing What to Collect* starts with that moment. Write down the decision the data will inform. For each candidate source, record what it covers, who supplied it, which version you read, and what uses its terms allow. If the group needs trees along public streets, the front-yard question matters. If the next step is to publish a map, the terms matter too. Toronto, for example, asks users of information under its Open Government Licence to acknowledge the source. That is something a volunteer can record on the day they choose a dataset. [Toronto’s licence](https://open.toronto.ca/open-data-licence/)

The same discipline applies to writing a research paper. In the original survey, 76 entries had source addresses. QuoteLedger could trace those leads, but none included an exact passage. In a separate check for the public edition, six narrowly stated claims matched text on the relevant publishers’ or maintainers’ pages. One was Toronto’s attribution requirement. Another was CERN’s statement about which levels of event data its portal focuses on. Each check answers a question a reader can revisit. [Paper, section 12.6](https://github.com/OneiroNexus/choosing-what-to-collect/blob/v0.5/paper/PAPER_v0.5_2026-11-22.md#126-checks-on-the-public-edition)

The paper proposes four tests. One asks what happens to task performance when you remove records that serve no written goal. A drop in performance would be worth knowing. It could mean those records carried information the selection rule missed. That experiment is still open.

For the neighborhood group, a result worth sharing could be a map. It could also be a two-sentence note explaining why an attractive inventory was set aside. Either gives the next volunteer a place to start.
